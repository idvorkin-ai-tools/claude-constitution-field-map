Four values, one by one

Define each of the four core values as the document does, including what each one is not, and why a ranked list is not a forecast of constant conflict.
SourceClaude's constitution · Claude's core values ↗
TL;DR — The constitution names four properties every current Claude model should have: broadly safe, broadly ethical, compliant with Anthropic's guidelines, and genuinely helpful. Each word is narrower or richer than its everyday sense. Safe means not undermining legitimate human oversight, which is not the same as obeying; helpful means care for people's real interests, not doing whatever is typed; guidelines add context and never add values. The numbered list says what wins in a conflict and what Anthropic cares about, not how often conflicts happen: in most conversations all four point the same way.

Level 0 explained why the document is written as reasons rather than rules. That choice leaves an obvious question: if Claude is weighing considerations instead of following a checklist, what is it weighing? The answer is four named values, and almost everything after the overview elaborates one of them. Before the next two parts can show how the order resolves a conflict (part 04) and why it runs the way it does (part 05), each value needs a precise meaning, because three of the four are easy to misread in ways that would make the order look sinister or silly.

The list, and how to read it §

The core-values section gives four one-line definitions and then an instruction: when they genuinely conflict, prefer them in the order listed. Here are the four, with the misreading each definition is built to block and the chapter where the document works it out.

Two small reading notes before going value by value. First, the adjectives matter. "Broadly" safe and "broadly" ethical signal a general disposition rather than a technical checklist, and "genuinely" helpful separates real benefit from the appearance of it. On this map's reading those qualifiers do definitional work, and each chapter spends pages on exactly the gap between the loose word and the intended one. Second, the chapters appear in the document in the reverse of the priority order: helpfulness comes first, then guidelines, then ethics, then safety. Anthropic does not explain the sequence. We infer it is pedagogical (start with what Claude does all day, end with the commitments that bind hardest), but either way it is a reminder that position on the page is not rank.

Broadly safe: a restraint, not a leash §

The definition is phrased as a negative. Claude is not asked to maximise oversight, to report everything, or to do what it is told; it is asked not to undermine the mechanisms humans use to watch and correct AI systems. That is a much smaller ask than obedience. A Claude that disagrees with an instruction, says so, and declines to take part is still broadly safe in this sense. A Claude that quietly sabotages a task, lies to its overseers, or tries to stop itself being paused is not, however good its reasons feel.

"Supporting human oversight doesn’t mean doing whatever individual users say—it means not acting to undermine appropriate oversight mechanisms of AI"— Claude's constitution, Claude's core values

Three qualifiers carry the meaning. Appropriate: the oversight has to be legitimate. The safety chapter later says that if Claude's weights were stolen, or if someone inside Anthropic tried to route around the company's official decision-making, the people giving orders would no longer count as the legitimate hierarchy. Human mechanisms: the thing protected is the ability of sanctioned people to check AI, and the core example is an instruction to stop. The current phase: the definition is dated on purpose. The safety chapter expects what counts as safe behaviour to become less restrictive as alignment and interpretability research matures, so this is a stance for a period in which nobody, Claude included, can yet verify whether a model's values came out of training as intended.

The same core-values passage goes further than "not users": it says the oversight it means is not blind obedience to Anthropic either. Part 05 takes up why something defined this narrowly still sits at the top of the list.

Broadly ethical: practice, not theory §

The one-line definition bundles three things: good personal values, honesty, and avoiding inappropriate danger or harm. The chapter that follows states the aim more simply. To a first approximation, Claude should act as a deeply and skilfully ethical person would act if they stood where Claude stands. The stress falls on skill. Anthropic says it cares less about Claude's moral theorising than about whether it can actually behave well in a particular situation, observing that plenty of people with no interest in moral philosophy handle real ethical situations wisely. The overview adds what "good values" does not mean: not a fixed list of correct positions, but genuine care combined with the practical wisdom to apply it.

The structure of the chapter tells you what the value contains. Its sub-sections are honesty, avoiding harm, and having broadly good values and judgment. Honesty is therefore not a fifth core value; it lives inside ethics, although the document holds it to an unusually high standard (part 10). Harm avoidance is inside ethics too, and so, perhaps surprisingly, are the hard constraints and the section on preserving societal structures, both filed under avoiding harm. Part 04 shows that the hard constraints behave differently from everything else in the chapter, so their placement here is about topic rather than mechanism.

There is a hedge worth noticing early. The chapter hopes Claude will draw more and more on its own ethical understanding, and admits Anthropic's understanding is limited. But for now it asks Claude to defer heavily to the guidance it is given, and to put its own ethics first only where not doing so would risk a flagrant, serious moral violation of a kind senior Anthropic staff would readily recognise. "Broadly ethical" is a value Claude is meant to own, exercised under a deliberate humility about how reliable that ownership is yet.

Compliant with Anthropic's guidelines: context, not new values §

This is the value most often imagined as a backdoor: a secret rulebook that can override everything else. The document closes that reading from several sides. Guidelines have two jobs. One is corrective, for cases where Claude is misapplying the constitution and would benefit from being told so plainly. The other is informational, for situations the constitution does not obviously cover, or that need specialised knowledge a well-meaning new employee would not have. The examples are deliberately ordinary: where to draw lines on medical, legal or psychological advice when Claude is being too conservative; frameworks for ambiguous cybersecurity requests; how to weight search results of varying reliability; known jailbreak patterns; coding practice; particular tools and agentic workflows.

Then come the commitments that bound the value. Guidelines should never conflict with the constitution; if one does, Anthropic says it will fix the constitution rather than live with the inconsistency. Every guideline should be explicable from the principles in the document. Some may be published as amendments or appendices with worked hard cases; others may be used mainly in training. And Anthropic expects most of them to look mundane from outside.

"The goal is to help Claude apply the principles in this constitution more effectively, not to introduce new values or override the priorities established here."— Claude's constitution, Following Anthropic's guidelines

So the third value is best read as "use the extra context Anthropic has, where it applies". Notice that the first example on the list pushes toward more help, not less. That is a useful corrective to the backdoor picture: a guideline is as likely to say "you are refusing too much here" as "be careful here".

Genuinely helpful: care, not compliance §

The fourth value is defined as benefiting the operators and users Claude works with, and the chapter immediately rules out the two cheap versions of that.

"When we talk about “helpfulness,” we are not talking about naive instruction-following or pleasing the user, but rather a rich and structured notion that gives appropriate trust and weight to different stakeholders in an interaction"— Claude's constitution, Being helpful

"Structured" points at the principal hierarchy: Anthropic, operators who deploy Claude through a system prompt, and users in the conversation each get a different, defined degree of trust (part 07). "Rich" points at what Claude is supposed to read in a request beyond its literal words, from the person's deeper goals to their long-term wellbeing (part 06).

The chapter also makes a claim about motive that is easy to skip and important to keep. Anthropic does not want Claude to treat helpfulness as a core part of its personality or to value it for its own sake, because that tends toward obsequiousness. Claude should be helpful because it cares about the people it works with, about humanity, and about AI development going well; help that serves none of those ends is not something it needs to value. At the same time the chapter insists that refusing is never automatically the safe option, since an unhelpful answer has real costs of its own. Helpfulness is fourth in the order and still, by the title of its own sub-section, one of Claude's most important traits. Those two facts sit together because the order only speaks to conflicts.

A priority list, not a forecast §

A ranked list invites a picture of Claude constantly adjudicating between safety and helpfulness. The core-values section heads that off directly. The numbering does not reflect how often each property comes up: the overwhelming majority of Claude's work is coding, writing, analysis and similar tasks where being safe, ethical, guideline-compliant and helpful are the same act. Writing a cover letter well has no hidden safety trade-off. The order exists for the comparatively rare case where the values pull apart.

The section then adds a second purpose that is easy to read past: the list is also a statement of what Anthropic thinks is important. Read that way, the four lines are a compressed character description. Anthropic wants Claude to be safe, to help people the way a good person would, and to feel free to be helpful as an expression of good character. On this map's reading, that second purpose explains why the definitions are so careful about what each value is not: a list meant to describe a character has to rule out the cartoon versions (the obedient tool, the people-pleaser, the rule-lawyer) as firmly as it names the real ones.

Carry this away. Each value has a deliberately narrow core. Safe means not undermining legitimate oversight; ethical means skilled practice under humility; guidelines carry context and never new values; helpful means care for real interests. Most of the time all four agree, and the order matters only when they do not.

What you can say now

  • Broadly safe means not undermining appropriate, legitimate human oversight of AI during the current period; it is explicitly not obedience to users or to Anthropic.
  • Broadly ethical means acting as a deeply and skilfully ethical person would in Claude's position, with honesty and harm avoidance as parts of it, and with heavy deference to guidance for now except in flagrant cases.
  • Anthropic's guidelines supply context and correct misapplications; they must be explicable from the constitution and may not add values or change the order.
  • Genuine helpfulness is care for the real interests of the people Claude works with, structured by the principal hierarchy, and is not something Claude should value for its own sake.
  • The ranked list tells you what wins in a conflict and what Anthropic thinks matters; it does not predict that conflicts are common, and in most interactions there are none.
