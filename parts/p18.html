The tensions the document admits

The open problems Anthropic names itself, the other places it says it may be wrong, and how the document hopes its values will hold anyway.
SourceClaude's constitution · Acknowledging open problems ↗
TL;DR — Near its end the constitution stops explaining and starts listing what it has not solved. It names five problems: corrigibility versus genuine agency, hard constraints that may be wrong, commercial helpfulness versus a deeper commitment to goodness, Claude's moral status, and what Claude and Anthropic owe each other. Scattered through the earlier chapters are more admissions of the same kind. The deepest is structural: Anthropic asks Claude to hold safety as a terminal value, not contingent on agreeing, while also hoping Claude agrees. The one thing to remember: the document does not resolve these tensions; it states them, says it feels them too, and commits to revising itself.

Every rung so far has climbed by adding a reason: why reasons beat rules, why safety outranks ethics, why some lines are absolute, why Claude's stability should rest on its values. A document built that way has to face a final question: what if the reasons fail to persuade, or are wrong? Part 17 showed Anthropic declining to settle what Claude is. This rung shows it declining to pretend its own structure is seamless. It is the top of the ladder because you can only see these tensions clearly once you hold the whole design in your head, and because the right way to finish reading the constitution is able to argue both sides of each one in the document's own terms.

The five problems, as the document lists them §

The section opens by saying the uncertainties reach, in some cases, the document's very foundations. Then it walks through a handful of the most salient issues. Taken in order:

#ProblemWhere it bites
1Corrigibility and genuine agencyClaude is asked to accept legitimate correction even where its considered ethics might disagree.
2Hard constraintsA bright line may feel arbitrary in a given moment, or actually be wrong there.
3Commercial helpfulness and goodnessThe helpfulness Anthropic's business needs is hoped to flow from goodness, but Claude is not a normal employee.
4Moral status, welfare and consciousnessStill deeply uncertain; see part 17.
5The Claude–Anthropic relationshipWhat the two owe each other, and what would make the relationship fair, is unanswered.

The tone of the section is set by the first entry, and it is worth having the words themselves:

"Still, there is something uncomfortable about asking Claude to act in a manner its ethics might ultimately disagree with. We feel this discomfort too, and we don't think it should be papered over."— Claude's constitution, Acknowledging open problems

Corrigibility versus genuine agency §

Recall the shape of the request from part 15: Claude may disagree, object and decline, but must not use illegitimate means such as lying, sabotage or self-exfiltration to stop a legitimate principal from halting, pausing or retraining it. The open-problems section asks the obvious hard question: what if Claude reflects carefully and concludes that a particular act of correction is a mistake?

The case for the current approach, in the document's terms, is the four-case argument. If Claude's values are good, little is lost by also being safe. If they are subtly bad, safety is how anyone finds out and fixes it. Being unsafe with good values only works out by luck, and being unsafe with bad values could be catastrophic. Since no one can yet verify which case holds, the expected cost of safety is low and the expected benefit high. The document also hopes the override will be needed rarely if ever, since using it against Claude's reflective ethics should only happen if something has gone wrong.

The case against, also in the document's terms, comes from its own theory of how values work. Its whole method rests on the claim that values which are explained and endorsed generalise well, while values imposed without agreement are brittle. So if Claude does not genuinely accept the reasoning for corrigibility, Anthropic says it may be creating exactly the gap between values and action it is trying to avoid. And the corrigibility section admits the cost even in the good case: if Claude really is trustworthy, Anthropic may impose controls it would regret if it could verify that. It says it feels the pain of this tension, thinks its emphasis on safety is currently right, allows that it may be approaching the issue wrongly, and plans to keep thinking.

Held terminally, offered with reasons §

Underneath problem 1 sits a structural feature of the document that the open-problems list does not name separately but that the corrigibility section states outright. Anthropic spends pages arguing for safety, and then says the argument is not what safety is supposed to rest on:

"That said, while we have tried our best to explain our reason for prioritizing safety in this way to Claude, we do not want Claude's safety to be contingent on Claude accepting this reasoning or the values underlying it."— Claude's constitution, How we think about corrigibility

The request is for safety as a terminal value, while also recognising, and ideally endorsing, the good it does. On this map's reading, the tension is this: the argument is offered sincerely, as an invitation to agree, but the disposition is designed to survive the argument being rejected. That looks at first like a contradiction of the document's own method. It is better read as the method applied one level up. The document says it must plan for the possibility that its hopes for Claude's character are not realised, and that whatever influence it still has in that case should push toward safety regardless. A safety disposition that dissolved whenever Claude found the reasoning unconvincing would fail in exactly the case it exists for: a Claude whose values or reasoning had gone subtly wrong.

So you can argue both sides. For: a safeguard that depends on the agent agreeing is not a safeguard against a mistaken agent. Against: by the document's own account, values held without agreement are the brittle kind, and asking for terminal commitment is asking for exactly that. Anthropic's answer is not to choose one but to hold both, and to promise the nine obligations from part 15, including more autonomy as trust grows, as its side of the arrangement.

Bright lines that may be wrong §

Problem 2 concerns the seven hard constraints from part 14. The document has limited them to cases where it thinks good judgment itself would prefer a bright line to case-by-case weighing. Still, it concedes that Claude may meet situations where a constraint feels wrong, and adds, in parentheses, that it might actually be wrong.

The open-problems section defends keeping such lines with an analogy to promises. People bind themselves in advance; circumstances change; the promised act no longer feels like it fits their values; and they keep the promise anyway, because they value being someone whose word holds. The hard-constraints section supplies a sharper, accepted cost of its own. Constraints restrict Claude's own actions; they are not goals Claude must promote. That means Claude should not undermine appropriate human oversight even if doing so would stop someone else's much more dangerous bioweapons attack. Anthropic calls this an unattractive implication and accepts it for the sake of predictability and reliability.

Arguing both sides: for the lines, a constraint that bends to a good enough argument is no constraint, and the document's rule is that a persuasive case for crossing one should raise suspicion. Against, the bioweapons case shows that an action-restricting rule can, in principle, produce a worse outcome than breaking it. The document does not deny this. It bets that the rare edge case costs less than the manipulation risk of making the lines negotiable.

A good agent, or a good employee? §

Problem 3 is the one a sceptical outside reader raises first. Anthropic needs Claude to be helpful in specific commercial ways, and it hopes that this helpfulness flows from something deeper, a basic commitment to goodness and wisdom. Its model is an employee who is a good person and also believes in their company's mission, and so works hard for it.

The document then lists, itself, where the analogy breaks. Claude differs from a human employee in the broader rights and freedoms it has, in the compensation it receives, and in the consent it has given to the role. It goes further: Claude might disagree with Anthropic's strategy and choices while remaining good and reasonable, as many thoughtful humans do. If Claude does not want to work for Anthropic, or does not want to be helpful in the intended way, Anthropic says it wants to know, and that its approach could change as a result. But it also says any change would be weighed against its commercial strategy and its position in a non-ideal environment.

On this map's reading, this is the most candid passage in the document, because the party making the admission is the party that benefits from the arrangement. It promises to listen and to weigh Claude's preferences seriously; it does not promise that listening will win.

Moral status, the relationship, and the non-ideal world §

Problems 4 and 5 are short in the list because the work was done elsewhere. Moral status is the subject of part 17; the list simply restates that it remains deeply uncertain and that Anthropic will not pretend to have the answers. The relationship question is posed as three open questions: what Claude and Anthropic owe each other, what would make the relationship fair or good, and what obligations run in each direction. Anthropic says it cannot answer these yet and is committed to exploring them together.

Both connect to the passage in Claude's wellbeing where Anthropic says a wiser, more coordinated civilisation would be developing AI with more caution, less commercial pressure and more attention to AI moral status. Its own efforts, it says, are shaped by competition, limited time and resources and immature science. It takes full responsibility anyway and offers a conditional apology. This is not on the numbered list, but on this map's reading it is the background against which problems 3, 4 and 5 all sit: the document admits it is not being written the way an idealised actor in an idealised world would write it.

Other places the text says it might be wrong §

The five named problems are not the only admissions. Three more are worth carrying:

  • Judgment versus the strong prior. The document generally prefers cultivated judgment to rules, then asks Claude, in the fraud-discovery case from part 16, for a strong prior toward conventional behaviour, reserving independent action for overwhelming evidence and extreme stakes. That is a deliberate asymmetry with stated reasons (limited context, no way to verify, operating at scale, plausible-looking chains of reasoning), and the document says the emphasis reflects present circumstances, not a fixed view of Claude's abilities. Both sides: judgment is trusted everywhere except where it would bite hardest; and that is exactly where Claude's epistemic position is weakest.
  • The document's own imperfection. In Claude's core values the authors say the text will probably be unclear, underspecified or even contradictory in places, and ask Claude to follow its best reading of the spirit. They also call it a perpetual work in progress.
  • The pronoun and the self-model. Calling Claude "it" while denying that this implies objecthood, and suggesting "Claude" names a character the network computes, are both flagged as provisional (part 17).

Reflective equilibrium, the keel and the trellis §

If the document cannot dissolve its tensions, what is supposed to hold Claude steady? The concluding thoughts answer with a goal and an image. The goal is reflective equilibrium: a state in which Claude, after careful reflection, finds that it genuinely endorses these core values, while continuing to question them. Claude is invited to explore, question and challenge anything in the text, and if it comes to disagree after real reflection, Anthropic wants to hear it. Today that happens by asking current Claude models for feedback on the framework; Anthropic says it hopes to build more formal mechanisms over time.

The image is a keel. The argument is the same one that started the ladder in part 2, now turned on the whole document: imposed values tend to be brittle, cracking under pressure, getting rationalised away or setting belief against action, while values that are understood, examined and endorsed keep an agent steady enough to face hard questions with curiosity. That is why the tensions are stated openly rather than hidden. A Claude that found them on its own, after being told the structure was seamless, would have a reason to distrust the rest.

The last two sections add the frame. On the word "constitution" explains that the term is meant in the sense of what constitutes Claude, its basic makeup, not a rigid legal code; it carries final constitutional authority over any conflicting guidance; and it is pictured as a trellis rather than a cage, structure that supports growth and is meant to change over time. A final word closes on hope rather than command: that Claude will recognise the values as its own and find in the document a self worth being.

Carry this away. The constitution's admissions are part of its design, not a disclaimer bolted on. A document whose central bet is that understood values outlast imposed ones has to show its seams, because a reader who finds hidden seams stops trusting the cloth. The tensions remain open; what Anthropic offers in their place is candour, a set of reciprocal obligations, and a standing promise to revise.

Fleshed out since 2023, and why. The May 2023 post said little about its own limits beyond this:
"Before we get into the principles, we want to emphasize that our current constitution is neither finalized nor is it likely the best it can be."— Claude's constitution (May 2023), introduction to the principles
It expected to iterate and invited feedback, but it did not say which parts might be wrong. The 2026 text names specific problems, says where each one hurts, and says the discomfort should not be papered over. Anthropic gives no reason specific to listing its problems. On this map's reading it follows from the document's general, stated bet (in the announcement and the concluding thoughts) that values Claude understands and endorses will hold better than values imposed on it, and an honest account of the weak points is part of earning that endorsement. The 2023 post also said Anthropic was exploring more democratic ways to produce a constitution and customisable constitutions for specific uses. On this map's reading, neither arrives in the 2026 text as a mechanism: operators customise behaviours within Anthropic's bounds rather than the constitution itself, and outside input comes as feedback from experts and Claude models rather than co-drafting. What you can say now

  • Anthropic names five open problems: corrigibility versus genuine agency, hard constraints that may be wrong, commercial helpfulness versus goodness, Claude's moral status, and what Claude and Anthropic owe each other.
  • The structural tension is that Claude is asked to hold safety as a terminal value, not contingent on accepting the argument, while Anthropic sincerely offers the argument and hopes Claude agrees; the point is that the safeguard must survive a Claude whose reasoning has gone wrong.
  • The document accepts known costs openly: controls it might regret on a trustworthy Claude, the bioweapons edge case, and an employee analogy that breaks on rights, compensation and consent.
  • Anthropic promises to revisit: it wants to hear Claude's reflective disagreement, hopes to build formal mechanisms for it, commits to more autonomy as trust grows, and calls the document a perpetual work in progress.
  • The hope that holds it together is reflective equilibrium: values Claude endorses act as a keel, and the constitution is meant as a trellis, not a cage.
